Before you start

The argument in brief

Perkovich asks three questions pp. 5–8, 444. Why did India develop nuclear weapons when and how it did? What keeps India from stopping or reversing course? What effect did the United States have? The usual answer to the first is security: China tested in 1964, so India built a bomb. Perkovich shows that this does not fit the record. India took ten years to test after China did, declined to build weapons for a further fourteen, and tested again in 1998 when its security was no worse than before.

His explanation rests on domestic factors: the moral legacy of Gandhi and Nehru, the wish for standing in the world, the cost, a handful of individual leaders, and above all the scientists he calls the strategic enclave, who built the capability first and sought permission afterwards. The conclusion sets out four “illusions” that India’s history explodes.

Illusion 1Security concerns decisively determine proliferation. p. 446
Illusion 2Nonproliferation is the flip side of the proliferation coin: remove the cause and the programme goes away. p. 455
Illusion 3Democracy facilitates nonproliferation. p. 459
Illusion 4Equitable disarmament is unnecessary for nonproliferation. p. 464

Where the book speaks of status. If you are reading for standing, rank and recognition, these are the passages to mark. Bhabha and Nehru saw nuclear technology as a shortcut to modernity and major power status p. 59. The Enterprise episode of 1971 mattered as a problem of esteem p. 166. The desire for major power status, more than any other international consideration, drove the wish to demonstrate the capability in 1974 p. 168. By 1978 nuclear energy had become an issue of national prestige more than of defence p. 216. China held the rising great power status India coveted p. 290. A BJP spokesman in 1993: nuclear weapons will give prestige, power, standing p. 334. In a 1994 elite poll, 38 per cent of bomb supporters wanted it to enhance India’s international status and only 20 per cent cited China p. 354. Equity and rank at the unofficial Goa meeting of 1995 pp. 356–358. Gujral’s story of the sign on the Security Council door p. 400. The seven aims of the 1998 tests, beginning with recognition as a major power p. 439. Standing and autonomy explain the tests better than security does, yet economic strength is what now confers rank pp. 441–442. The two norms that pull against each other, major power status and moral superiority pp. 448–449. The six factors p. 452. The wish to be treated as an equal among the major powers pp. 465–466.

Part two

The machinery, one piece at a time

The lessons are in the order you need them. Each says what the thing is, how it works, and where it turns up in the book.

1. Fission, chain reactions and critical mass

Some very heavy atoms split when a neutron hits them. Each split releases energy and two or three new neutrons. If each of those goes on to split another atom, the process feeds itself. That is a chain reaction. background

Whether the chain grows or dies depends on a race. A neutron either finds another nucleus or escapes through the surface of the metal. In a small or loosely packed lump most escape. In a larger or denser one most hit something. The point where the chain just sustains itself is called critical. Perkovich puts it in a sentence: once the plutonium is dense enough, injected neutrons “collide with and preferentially split plutonium atoms rather than escape” p. 140.

Try it · fire a neutron into the metal, then squeeze the metal and fire again

With the slider at the left, fire several neutrons. A few atoms split and the chain fizzles. Move the slider right to squeeze the same atoms into a smaller space and fire once. This is the whole idea of the implosion bomb: you do not add material, you crush what is there.

2. Two roads to bomb material

A bomb needs fissile material, and making it is the hard, slow, expensive part of any programme. There are two ways. India chose one and Pakistan the other, and that difference runs through the whole book.

From uranium ore to a bomb core

3. Inside a reactor: how CIRUS made plutonium

A reactor is a chain reaction held steady. In CIRUS, the 40-megawatt research reactor Canada offered in 1955, the fuel is natural uranium metal and the neutrons are slowed by heavy water p. 27. Canada asked only for a pledge of peaceful use, in a secret annex, with no inspections worth the name. Indian engineers hurried to make the first fuel load themselves so that India could claim the plutonium as its own p. 27.

A heavy-water reactor at work

Why heavy water mattered. India’s own heavy water plants were late and unreliable. The reactors could not start without it, so in 1982 and 1983 India bought some 60 tonnes of Chinese-origin heavy water in secret through a German broker, Alfred Hempel, with more from Norway and the Soviet Union p. 250.

Dhruva. A second, larger reactor at Trombay: 100 megawatts, Indian-built, outside safeguards, started in 1985 p. 271. It was meant to add twenty to twenty-five kilograms of plutonium a year, though early faults kept it shut for long periods p. 285.

Why the grade mattered in 1998. Perkovich reports that one of the 1998 devices, the low-yield one fired on 11 May, used plutonium that was not weapon-grade pp. 404, 428. India’s weapon-grade stock was then perhaps 250 to 300 kilograms, enough for about fifty weapons. If power reactor plutonium can be used as well, the usable stock is far larger: roughly 600 kilograms more at the end of 1998 p. 430. The drawback is that the unwanted isotopes throw off stray neutrons that can start the chain too early p. 429.

4. Reprocessing: getting the plutonium out

Plutonium made in a reactor is locked inside used fuel, mixed with uranium and fiercely radioactive waste. Separating it is a chemical job done by remote control behind concrete. Bhabha decided in 1958 to build a plant for it at Trombay, called Phoenix p. 28. A country that can reprocess has crossed the line from having reactors to having bomb material, which is why American law later singled out reprocessing and enrichment for sanctions p. 198.

The Purex process in five steps

5. Bhabha’s three-stage plan

This plan is the reason plutonium could be pursued openly for twenty years. It promised cheap, limitless electricity from Indian thorium, and each stage needed the plutonium or uranium-233 made by the one before. Perkovich lists its flaws: reactors too small to be economic, heavy water that had to be imported, and no serious cost analysis pp. 32–33. In August 1961 Bhabha’s commission forecast 3,000 megawatts of nuclear electricity by 1976. Thirty-seven years on India had little more than half that p. 38.

Uranium to plutonium to thorium

6. “Peaceful nuclear explosions”

In the 1950s and 1960s the United States ran a programme called Plowshare to use nuclear explosions for civil engineering: digging harbours and canals, freeing gas from rock. The Soviet Union had its own. This gave the idea of a peaceful explosion real standing, and Bhabha raised Plowshare with American officials in 1965 p. 96.

The device for a peaceful explosion is a bomb. Nothing in the physics tells them apart; only the stated purpose differs. That made the phrase politically useful. It let Shastri in 1964 give the scientists something without breaking with Gandhi and Nehru, and it let Indira Gandhi in 1974 test while saying India had “no intention of producing nuclear weapons” p. 178. The NPT refuses the distinction and treats any nuclear explosive device as a weapon. India’s insistence on the distinction, and everyone else’s refusal of it, is one of the book’s running disputes.

7. The implosion device

This is the heart of the technical story. A plutonium bomb cannot simply slam two pieces together, because plutonium would start reacting too soon and blow itself apart weakly. It has to be squeezed from all sides at once, very fast. Perkovich describes the principle on p. 140 and the Indian team’s work on it across chapters 5 to 7.

Cross-section of an implosion device · step through, then tick the box and run it again

The equation of state. To design the squeeze you must know how plutonium behaves at millions of atmospheres and millions of degrees: how its pressure, density and temperature relate. Nobody publishes that. In late 1967 or early 1968 Ramanna gave the problem to R. Chidambaram, then thirty-two, who worked it out from scarce data on related elements pp. 140–141. Without it one cannot say how much explosive is needed or what the yield will be.

Purnima. The designers also wanted to measure how fast neutrons behave in plutonium. In 1970 they began a tiny pulsed fast reactor at Trombay, with no budget, from parts begged from other divisions. It first ran in May 1972. Perkovich notes that a simple fission weapon works on the same principle as such a reactor, except that in a bomb the pulse is very big pp. 149–150.

Who built what. No more than seventy-five people took part. Chidambaram and S. K. Sikka designed the device. Iyengar’s group made the initiator. The Terminal Ballistics Research Laboratory in Chandigarh made the explosive system. Dastidar and Seshadri made the electronics p. 172.

Why symmetry is everything. If the charges do not fire together, the core is squashed out of shape, compresses less, and wastes its plutonium. Perkovich judges that inexact simultaneity and symmetry were the most likely cause of the 1974 device’s poor yield p. 183. The fix could be tested without plutonium, which is how India improved its design for twenty-four years without a nuclear test pp. 183, 294.

8. The initiator, code-named Flower

The chain reaction needs a first burst of neutrons at exactly the right instant. In the 1974 device this came from a part one and a half centimetres long at the centre of the core. It held polonium and beryllium on either side of a barrier. When the implosion crushed it, a jet of beryllium shot into the polonium, and the mixture gave off neutrons p. 173.

Polonium was the trouble. It is made by irradiating bismuth that is 99.999 per cent pure in a reactor. It is so radioactive that it ate through the stainless steel and gold used to hold it p. 173. And it does not keep.

Polonium-210 decaying · drag the slider

This is why, once the polonium was ready in 1974, there was pressure to test quickly p. 173. It is also why a polonium initiator suits a one-off experiment and not an arsenal: it would need replacing every few months, in a laboratory p. 272. From the 1980s the designers worked on an initiator using deuterium and tritium, and Perkovich counts its proving in 1998 as a real advance p. 431.

9. The road to Pokhran, 1960 to 1974

Perkovich lists the steps a first device requires p. 171. Put in order with their dates they show how much was done before any prime minister formally said yes.

Fourteen years of preparation

The test itself cost Rs 32 lakh by Sethna’s count. One Indian estimate that included five years of research put the figure at Rs 1,760 million p. 181. The gap is the difference between the price of the last step and the price of the whole staircase.

10. What happens in an underground test

The 1963 Partial Test Ban Treaty, which India signed, forbids explosions in the air. So India tested underground, in desert near Pokhran. An underground shot hides the fireball but not the shock, and it leaves a cavity, a crater and radioactive debris that can be sampled later.

Cross-section of the test site

Seen from space. Digging and cabling a shaft is visible to satellites. In December 1995 American satellites picked up the activity and the story reached the New York Times p. 368. In 1998 the teams worked when satellites were not overhead p. 2. US satellites did record clear signs six hours before the shots, but the analysts were asleep p. 418.

Three at once. On 11 May 1998 three devices were fired together in shafts about a kilometre apart. One reason, Perkovich reports, was to blur the seismic signal so that outsiders could not separate the yields p. 426.

The best measurement. The most reliable way to find a yield is radiochemistry: drill into the cavity and analyse the debris. India did this in 1974 and never published the result p. 182.

Scale: 0 to 65 kilotons. The two tests of 13 May 1998 were announced at 0.2 and 0.6 kilotons p. 425.

Sethna told Perkovich in 1996 that the 1974 yield “was much lower than had been stated” p. 182. Perkovich’s point is institutional as much as technical: the aura of 1974 shielded the atomic establishment from scrutiny for two decades.

12. Levitation, boosting and the hydrogen bomb

After 1974 the designers’ aim was to get more explosion out of less weight, so that a device could fit under an aircraft or on a missile. The 1974 device weighed about 1,000 kilograms. By 1982 the team had a design of 170 to 200 kilograms p. 242. These are the three steps up, in the order the book meets them.

From a plain core to two stages

Where the ingredients came from. Tritium is made in reactors. India ran a pilot plant extracting it from heavy water p. 2. BARC is reported to have begun separating lithium-6 in the mid-1980s p. 427. In 1984 India imported nearly a hundred kilograms of pure beryllium from West Germany; a beryllium layer round the core reflects neutrons back in p. 271.

Why testing mattered here. A simple fission device can be trusted from laboratory work. Boosted and two-stage designs are much harder to trust without a full test. That is the technical core of the scientists’ case for testing before the test ban closed the door pp. 361, 365, 375.

The small shots. The two tests of 13 May were described as giving data for “improved computer simulation” p. 419. The official statement said the data would support computer simulation and, if needed, subcritical experiments p. 417. Subcritical experiments use nuclear material but stop short of a chain reaction. India had objected that the test ban left the established powers free to conduct them pp. 366, 379.

13. Pakistan’s road: the centrifuge

Pakistan first took the plutonium road and in October 1974 signed for a French reprocessing plant p. 186. In December 1974 A. Q. Khan, a metallurgist working in the Netherlands, urged Bhutto to enrich uranium instead, and a year later he stayed in Pakistan to do it pp. 195–196. Under American pressure France first demanded safeguards and in 1978 cancelled the plant p. 195, which left enrichment as the main route.

One centrifuge, then a cascade

Chapters 8 to 12 follow this effort from the Indian side. India watched it advance, considered bombing Kahuta, and found Washington unwilling to stop a Cold War ally. For Perkovich this weakens the simple security story in an unexpected way: Pakistan’s programme was itself a result of India’s 1971 victory and 1974 test pp. 165, 177.

14. Delivery: aircraft and missiles

A device in a shaft is not a weapon. A weapon has to be small and tough enough to be carried, and something has to carry it. The 1974 device was never designed to go on a rocket p. 183, and as late as 1983 India had not worked out how to deliver a nuclear weapon by air p. 249.

Three ways to deliver a warhead

Ranges to scale

Foreign intelligence concluded that Agni’s first flight covered only about 800 kilometres p. 301, and by 1998 it had been tested three times to about 1,000 p. 3. Pakistan’s Ghauri flew perhaps 800 kilometres on 6 April 1998; the 1,500 is Pakistan’s claim p. 409. A later Prithvi version reached 250 kilometres p. 331.

From space rocket to missile. India’s first satellite launcher, the SLV-3, was modelled on the American Scout rocket and reached orbit in July 1980. A rocket that can lift a satellite can lift a warhead p. 245. Abdul Kalam, who had worked on it, took charge of the missile effort in 1982 p. 246.

Five missiles at once. The 1983 programme developed the Nag, Trishul and Akash for the battlefield alongside Prithvi and Agni, so that each could borrow from the others pp. 246–247.

Supply before demand. Agni had no stated requirement from any armed service. It came, an engineer said, from the wish to do something with the capability available p. 248. Perkovich sees the same pattern as in the bomb itself: the technology arrives first and the strategy is found for it later.

15. Safeguards: what was inspected and what was not

Safeguards are the inspection system of the International Atomic Energy Agency. Inspectors count nuclear material going in and out of a plant, fit seals and cameras, and check the books, so that any diversion to weapons would show. They apply only where a state has agreed to them. India accepted them plant by plant, where a foreign supplier insisted, and nowhere else. The Americans and later all major suppliers wanted them everywhere: “full-scope” safeguards. This table, from the book’s appendix pp. 469–471, shows the result by 1998.

16. The treaties and American laws

The book is also a history of the nonproliferation regime, seen from the receiving end. Each entry below is a rule the book keeps returning to.

    17. The vocabulary of deterrence

    India’s capability changed faster than the words for it. These terms mark the positions the book describes.

    The option (1964 to late 1980s)Keep the means to build weapons and the right to do so. Build none. Say that India wants none.
    Recessed deterrence (about 1988 to 1998)Weapons exist as separated parts under civilian scientists’ control, to be assembled if needed. By 1994 the capability was “significant, albeit secret and recessed from military control” p. 349.
    Minimum deterrenceA small force for retaliation only. An informal committee in 1985 put its size at warheads in the low hundreds and its cost at Rs 7,000 crore over ten years p. 274.
    No first useA promise never to strike first with nuclear weapons. Part of the 1985 committee’s recommendation p. 274, urged by Rao in Washington in 1994 p. 347, and restated by Vajpayee’s office a week after the 1998 tests p. 420.
    Command and controlWho may order use, and how the order reaches a pilot or a missile crew. Through 1998 the armed forces were kept out of nuclear policy p. 404, and the tests were not guided by any new doctrine p. 4.

    18. Other technology the book mentions

    Supercomputers. India asked to buy an American Cray in the mid-1980s for weather forecasting. Washington hesitated because the same machine can model nuclear explosions and break codes. A less powerful model, the XMP-14, was sold; a faster XMP-22 was approved later pp. 269, 290, 302.

    Mixed-oxide fuel. A blend of plutonium and uranium oxides used as reactor fuel. In 1981 Sethna proposed to run Tarapur on it if American fuel stopped. Indira Gandhi doubted that it would work, and France became the supplier instead pp. 234–235. The appendix lists Tarapur 1 as using it p. 469.

    Fast breeder reactors. Reactors with no moderator, in which fast neutrons turn a surrounding blanket into new fuel. India’s test breeder at Kalpakkam started in 1985 p. 285.

    Laser enrichment. A way of separating uranium isotopes with tuned laser light. India had research sites at Trombay and Indore p. 471.

    Ring magnets. Precision magnets for the bearings that let a centrifuge rotor spin almost without friction. Reports in early 1996 that China had supplied 5,000 to Pakistan became a test of American resolve p. 373.

    Explosive lenses. Shaped blocks of fast and slow explosive that bend a blast wave into a sphere. The Indian teams drew on papers published by Los Alamos and Livermore p. 294.
